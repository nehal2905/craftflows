On a phone, your film's first frame competes with everything else in the feed. You get one line to make someone stop. That is why we write the hook before anything else in a script, then write twenty more, then keep the one we would stop for ourselves. Here are six openers from our films, the pattern behind each one, and how to borrow it.

## 1. The uncomfortable truth

> "Dental insurance isn't real insurance."

Denta opens by contradicting something the viewer assumes is true. A statement that sounds slightly wrong earns a second look, and the very next frame pays it off with a concrete example: the $1,000 crown where the patient still pays $500 or more out of pocket.

**Use it when** your product exists because a common belief is false.

**Borrow it:** "[Thing everyone trusts] isn't [what they think it is]."

## 2. The pile-up

> "A job cancels. A tech runs late. An urgent call comes in."

Fleety stacks three short sentences, each a small disaster for anyone who runs a dispatch board. The rhythm does the work: by the third line the viewer feels the pressure, and the film can introduce the fix.

**Use it when** your customer's problem is a chain of small things going wrong.

**Borrow it:** three short sentences, each a real moment from your customer's day.

## 3. The scattered life

> "Your places, plans and memories live in different apps."

Anywhere names a mess the viewer has never put into words. Recognition is one of the strongest reasons to stop scrolling: it feels like the film is talking about you. We broke this film down shot by shot in [Anatomy of a 15-second promo film](/blog/anatomy-of-a-15-second-promo-film).

**Use it when** your product brings together things that are currently spread out.

**Borrow it:** "Your [things] live in [too many places]."

## 4. The impossible job

> "Managers can't be everywhere."

Coach states a limit every sales manager lives with, then makes it concrete on the next line: "One manager. Ten reps. Hundreds of meetings." The numbers turn a general truth into a picture of the viewer's own week.

**Use it when** your buyer is stretched across more work than one person can do.

**Borrow it:** "[Role] can't [do the thing they're expected to do]."

## 5. The looks-fine trap

> "Your website might look great. But can customers find it?"

Theo starts with a compliment, then pulls the rug. The viewer agrees with the first half, which makes them listen to the second. It works especially well for products that fix a problem people don't know they have.

**Use it when** your customer thinks everything is fine.

**Borrow it:** "Your [asset] might [look good]. But [the real problem]?"

## 6. The missed moment

> "Your front desk is busy. Callers reach voicemail."

Kairos Health shows the cost happening in real time: a call comes in, nobody picks up, and a red "Missed call" lands on screen. Seeing the loss is more powerful than being told about it.

**Use it when** your product stops money or customers from slipping away.

**Borrow it:** show the exact moment the customer, the booking or the sale is lost.

## How to write your own hook

1. **Start with your customer's words, not yours.** Read reviews, support tickets and sales call notes. The best hooks are sentences customers already say.
2. **Lead with them.** Open with "Your", a role, or a situation. Not "We", and not your brand name.
3. **Keep it short.** Two lines on a phone screen, about ten words, big enough to read at arm's length.
4. **Write twenty.** Read them out loud and keep the one you would stop for.
5. **Pay it off in the next frame.** A hook the film never answers feels like bait, and people punish bait by scrolling.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout__label">The two-second test</p>
<p>Show only your first frame, on a phone, for two seconds, to someone outside your company. Then ask who it is for and what problem it is about. If they can't answer both, rewrite the hook before you make anything else.</p>
</div>

## Hooks we avoid

- **Questions with an easy "no".** "Want to grow your business?" invites a shrug.
- **The logo first.** It tells people "this is an ad" before they have a reason to care.
- **"Introducing..."** It is about you, not them.
- **Hype without a subject.** "This changes everything" says nothing about what changes or for whom.

## The short version

The hook is the cheapest part of a film to get right and the most expensive part to get wrong. Write it first, write it in your customer's words, and make sure the next frame pays it off. All six patterns are playing in [our films](/work), and the [one-message rule](/blog/one-message-promo-video) covers what comes after the hook.
